Silicon

The Backbone of Modern Technology

A Group 14 tetravalent metalloid that bridges the gap between conductors and insulators, enabling integrated circuits, photovoltaics, and computing.

Atomic Profile

Silicon sits in Group 14 of the periodic table, directly below carbon. Like carbon, it forms four covalent bonds, adopting a diamond-cubic crystal lattice in its solid state.

  • Atomic Number 14
  • Standard Atomic Weight 28.085 u
  • Electron Configuration [Ne] 3s² 3p²
  • Electronegativity 1.90 (Pauling scale)
  • Density (at room temp) 2.3290 g/cm³
  • Melting Point 1414 °C (1687 K)

Semiconductor Physics

In its pure (intrinsic) crystalline form, silicon is a mediocre conductor. Its valence band is full and its conduction band is empty, separated by an energy gap.

  • Bandgap Energy (Eg) 1.12 eV (300 K)
  • Bandgap Type Indirect Bandgap
  • Electron Mobility ~1400 cm²/(V·s)
  • Hole Mobility ~450 cm²/(V·s)
  • Crystal Lattice Diamond Cubic (a = 5.43 Å)
  • Electrical Classification Metalloid / Semiconductor

Doping: Controlling Electrical Conductivity

Pure silicon is rarely used in active electronic devices. By deliberately introducing trace impurity atoms into the crystalline lattice—a process known as doping—engineers can manipulate its electrical conductivity by several orders of magnitude.

n-Type Doping
Electron Donors (Group 15)

Introducing pentavalent atoms like Phosphorus (P) or Arsenic (As) adds a fifth valence electron that does not participate in covalent bonding, creating free negative charge carriers.

p-Type Doping
Electron Acceptors (Group 13)

Introducing trivalent atoms like Boron (B) or Gallium (Ga) leaves a missing electron in the lattice bond—a positive "hole" that moves through the crystal under an electric field.

p-n Junction
The Foundation of Logic

Joining p-type and n-type silicon creates a depletion region that allows current to flow in only one direction, forming diodes, transistors, and MOSFETs that power digital processors.

From Quartzite Sand to Single-Crystal Wafers

Silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust (by mass, after oxygen), occurring primarily as silicon dioxide (SiO₂) in sand and quartz. Transforming it into high-performance microchips requires extreme chemical and metallurgical purification.

Step 1
Carbothermic Reduction

Quartzite is heated with carbon in an electric arc furnace at ~1900 °C to strip away oxygen, yielding Metallurgical-Grade Silicon (~98–99% pure).

Step 2
Siemens Process

Silicon is converted to gas (trichlorosilane, SiHCl₃) and distilled to remove impurities, producing Polycrystalline Electronic-Grade Silicon (99.9999999% pure — "nine nines").

Step 3
Czochralski Crystal Growth

A seed crystal is dipped into molten silicon at 1425 °C and slowly pulled upward while rotating, forming a single-crystal monocrystalline ingot (boule).

Step 4
Wafer Slicing & Polishing

The ingot is sliced with diamond saws into thin discs (typically 300 mm wide), chemically etched, and polished to mirror flatness for integrated circuit fabrication.

Silicates & Ceramics

Over 90% of Earth's crust is composed of silicate minerals. Silicon dioxide (SiO₂) is the primary constituent of conventional glass, optical fibers, and structural ceramics, valued for thermal stability and chemical hardness.

Silicones & Polymers

When silicon is bonded with oxygen, carbon, and hydrogen, it forms synthetic polymers known as silicones (polysiloxanes). These elastomers offer high thermal resistance and are widely used in lubricants, medical implants, and sealants.
